Join us to explore the complex nature of microstructures through a unique intersection of applied mathematics, machine learning, and materials science
Job description
The properties of metals are shaped by their microstructures: intricate three-dimensional arrangements of grains, phases, and interfaces that emerge during processing. Understanding this internal architecture is key to designing better materials, optimising manufacturing processes, and developing predictive digital twins. Yet, despite major advances in materials characterisation and modelling, we still lack ways to capture the full complexity of microstructures in representations that are both mathematically rigorous and physically interpretable.
Can we develop new mathematical representations of metallic microstructures that capture their complexity while revealing their connection to material properties?
This is the central challenge of this PhD project. Working at the intersection of materials science, applied mathematics, and machine learning, you will explore new ways to describe, compare, and understand complex metallic microstructures. The ambition is to move beyond conventional descriptors, such as average grain size or phase fraction, while retaining the physical interpretability that is often missing from purely data-driven approaches.
You will investigate how the geometry, spatial organisation, topology, and crystallographic characteristics of microstructures can be captured in compact, information-rich representations. By combining mathematical insight with modern machine learning, the project aims to uncover the underlying organisation of microstructures: what makes two microstructures similar or different, which structural characteristics distinguish them, and how these characteristics relate to material properties.
A particularly exciting aspect of this project is the opportunity to bring together ideas and approaches from traditionally distinct disciplines. From statistical analysis, topological data analysis, and manifold learning to advanced microstructure characterisation and computational materials modelling, you will explore how different mathematical and physical perspectives can complement each other. Rather than simply applying existing techniques, you will have the opportunity to develop new connections between these fields, with the aim of establishing a more comprehensive and physically meaningful framework for understanding microstructures.
The project will focus primarily on steel microstructures, contributing to the development of more sustainable and efficient steel production. It will be embedded in a larger research consortium dedicated to green steel, providing opportunities to connect fundamental research with real-world challenges in materials production and processing. At the same time, the mathematical concepts developed in this project will have potential applications well beyond steel, across a broad range of polycrystalline metallic materials.
You will conduct your research at TU Delft, jointly embedded in the Department of Materials Science and Engineering and the Statistics Group of the Delft Institute of Applied Mathematics. This interdisciplinary environment offers a unique opportunity to develop fundamental mathematical and computational ideas while addressing challenging questions in physical metallurgy. You will benefit from expertise across both disciplines and opportunities to engage with academic and industrial partners throughout the metallic materials value chain.
